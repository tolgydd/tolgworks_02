The seminar takes the literary technique of *stream of consciousness* as a point of departure, exploring its potential implications for architectural thinking and design. In literature, stream of consciousness describes the continuous flow of a character's thoughts, associations, and perceptions, following the order in which they emerge without the intervention of an external narrator to organize or explain them. The narrative develops through the character's attention, experiences, and the relationships established between seemingly separate moments.

Drawing a parallel with architecture, the seminar proposes understanding spatial design through the sequence of experiences and activities of its intended users. Rather than approaching architectural organization as an arrangement of functions governed solely by established conventions, it considers how these conventions themselves emerge from patterns of use. The organization of a domestic interior, for instance, is not arbitrary: the relationship between activities, their order, proximity, and transitions establishes an underlying logic that informs spatial decisions. Understanding this sequence becomes essential to designing spaces that respond meaningfully to the ways people inhabit them.

The inquiry begins at the scale of interior architecture, examining how everyday functional requirements and established design principles can be understood through the narratives of use that generate them. Conventional arrangements in domestic spaces, particularly bathrooms and kitchens, provide examples of how spatial organization follows particular sequences of activities. The kitchen triangle, counter heights, and different kitchen configurations are considered not merely as technical rules, but as architectural responses to patterns of movement, preparation, interaction, and occupation. By understanding the activities that take place within a space and the relationships between them, the architect can derive an appropriate spatial organization rather than simply applying predefined solutions.

The discussion then moves to the scale of the building, examining early modernist residential architecture in Turkey through the same perspective. Historical residential projects, including the Villa Project in Kızıltoprak by Arif Hikmet, İzzet Bey House in Maçka by Mimar Saim, and Park Apartment in Nişantaşı by Ahmet İhsan, provide a basis for reading architectural plans as sequences of lived experiences. Their spatial arrangements, circulation patterns, thresholds, and functional relationships can be understood as narratives that follow the everyday routines of their inhabitants. Rather than reading plans merely as geometric compositions or collections of rooms, the seminar investigates the stories embedded within their organization: how one activity leads to another, how spaces relate to each other, and how architectural decisions anticipate the rhythms of domestic life.

Finally, the investigation extends to the urban scale through the notion of *paper architecture* and a selection of personal design projects. At this scale, the narrative approach expands beyond individual activities and domestic routines to encompass collective patterns of occupation, social relationships, and alternative ways of inhabiting the built environment. Through speculative drawings, visual narratives, collages, and architectural representations, these projects explore how spatial organization can emerge from imagined scenarios and sequences of use, opening up possibilities beyond existing architectural conventions.

Across these three scales, the seminar investigates architecture as a discipline of understanding and translating human experience into spatial organization. The stream of consciousness becomes a way of tracing the continuity between activities, perceptions, and spatial relationships, revealing the narratives that underpin architectural decisions. A successful architectural plan, in this sense, is not simply an efficient arrangement of functions, but one in which the relationships between spaces reflect an understanding of the experiences they are intended to accommodate.

Ultimately, the seminar proposes that architectural design begins with the ability to read and understand these narratives. Just as a literary narrative unfolds through the interconnected thoughts and experiences of a character, architecture can be conceived through the interconnected activities and experiences of its users. The architect's task is to recognize these sequences, understand their underlying logic, and translate them into spatial form.
